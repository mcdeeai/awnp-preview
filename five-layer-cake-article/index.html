The Myth of the Five-Layer Cake

By James McDonough

I want to tell you about a myth. I call it that because I can't prove it. A myth is true pieces, assembled by someone who wanted them to mean something.

And I think they do.

I've spent a lot of time with Jim Morgan. He's 87. He ran Applied Materials for nearly three decades, and in the semiconductor industry his name works like a passport. What he works on now is getting fifty years of management wisdom into the hands of nonprofit leaders.

On one of our calls, Jensen Huang came up. Jim likes Jensen. "One of the good ones," he said, and from Jim that's not a throwaway line. I like Jensen too, though from a different distance. To me, he may be the most interesting technology leader working today: clear, intense, hard to fake, and unusually good at turning complicated systems into language people can truly understand.

Then, offhand, the way you'd mention lending someone a ladder: something about layers, a frame he'd shared with Jensen years back, after Jensen heard Jim had written a book, or was writing one, and asked for some of the materials. An email, Jim thinks. Jim sent a stack. He doesn't remember what was in it.

That was the whole story, as far as Jim was concerned.

But he said layers, and something clicked.

If you follow AI at all, you've seen Jensen explain it: the five-layer cake. Energy, then chips, then infrastructure, then models, then applications on top, where the value shows up. He used it at GTC. He used it at Davos. It's now, more or less, how the world explains the AI economy to itself.

Jim hadn't seen it. But when he said layers, the clip came back to me all at once, and what I was thinking on my end of the phone was: of course this made sense to me after working with Jim for so long. A simple way of saying something very complicated could have come from the kind of materials Jim had been collecting for decades.

I think there's a decent chance the most famous explanatory frame in the AI industry spent time, somewhere in its childhood, in a stack of paper Jim Morgan mailed to a young CEO who asked.

Can I prove it? No. Does Jim claim it? No. When I floated it, he waved it off the porch. Would Jensen remember? Probably not, and that's no mark against him. Nobody keeps receipts on ideas. The ones you use become yours because you use them.

But look what the myth needs to be believable:

  1. A rising leader had the humility to ask a wise man for his materials.
  2. The wise man had materials ready, and sent them without ceremony.
  3. The idea mattered more than the credit. So much that neither man thought about it again.

Every one of those says something good about both men. Put the pieces together any way you want. Every version makes both men look good.

WHY I KEEP TELLING IT

Why I keep telling it

Because I work on the problem this myth is secretly about.

Up to 75% of nonprofit leaders plan to leave their positions within the next five to ten years. Only about a third of boards have a written succession plan. In cake terms, the top layer, decades of judgment and trust, is about to walk out the door, and almost nobody's written down the recipe.

ONE FOOT OUT THE DOOR

The myth is the solution in miniature. A leader asked. A leader had a stack ready. The stack traveled further than either of them could have imagined. That's the whole technology.

And Jim never stopped building his. His archive is folders, handwritten notes, sticky tabs, three-ring binders, going back decades. He calls it his collection of management tips, and he urges every leader to keep one. When Jensen asked, the stack was already on the shelf. That's the lesson hiding in the myth: the stack existed before the ask.

Jim grew up in a family cannery in Indiana, and that's the right image for what we're building at Applied Wisdom for Nonprofits: putting things up for winter. Sealing what's perishable into something that keeps, so it's on the shelf when someone comes asking.

Somebody is going to ask for your materials someday. Will you have a stack to send?
